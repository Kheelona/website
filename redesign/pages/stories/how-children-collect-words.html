How to build a 3-year-old’s vocabulary

How children pick up words at home, in every language you speak, without a single worksheet.

How can you build a 3-year-old’s vocabulary?

Talk with your child all through the day, and use real words, not only simple ones. Name what they point at, explain a new word as you use it (“this mango is ripe, so it’s soft”), read together, and wait for their answer. And count the words they know in every language you speak at home.[1]

Khelu is a toy, not a therapy or treatment. If you’re worried about your child’s speech, talk to your paediatrician.

Chances are nobody sat your child down to teach them “auto”, “gravy” or “cousin”. The words arrived anyway: from the kitchen, the market, the back seat and the family video call.

Here is what the research says about talk and words, and what you can do at home today.

What does the research say about talk and words?

In a 2018 study of 36 children aged 4 to 6, children who had more back-and-forth conversational turns with adults showed more activity in Broca’s area, a language region of the brain, while listening to a story. The number of adult words they heard showed no such link. Turns also predicted the children’s language scores once family income and parents’ education were taken into account.[2]

In the study, a turn was an adult speaking and the child replying, or the other way round. The children were native English speakers, and the study shows a link, not cause and effect.[2]

Its authors suggest encouraging parents to talk with their children in “more interactive, back-and-forth conversation”.[2] With a new word, one way to do that: say it, then give your child a reason to use it.

Everyday ways to build your child’s vocabulary

  • Use your real words: “enormous” and “delicious”, not only “big” and “nice”.
  • Explain a new word in the same breath: “The dal is boiling. See the bubbles?”
  • Name what your child points at, then wait for them to try the word.
  • Hand a new word back as a question: “It was enormous? How enormous?”
  • Read together, and talk about the pictures as well as the words.
  • Use a new word again over the next few days, somewhere new.
  • At meals, ask your child what they think, and let them finish.

None of this needs flashcards or a worksheet. It needs talk, a pause, and someone who listens to the answer.

Try this: pick one new word from your day, like “drizzle” or “crowded”, and use it at dinner, at bath time and at bedtime.

Any language counts

Counting your child’s vocabulary? Count every language. A bilingual child may split their words across two languages. Counted together, the total should be about the same as for a child of the same age learning one language.[1] Words collected in Telugu or Bangla count towards that total.[1]

A strong model in any language also helps build a foundation for learning others.[3] So keep talking in the languages you know best, every day.

For more on two languages at home, read Raising a bilingual child in India.

When should you ask your doctor?

Every child is different. As a general guide, if your child is 3 or older and other people still can’t understand them most of the time, or they lose words they used to have, talk to your paediatrician.[4]
