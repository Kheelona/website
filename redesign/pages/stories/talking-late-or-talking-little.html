3-year-old not talking in sentences? What to do

A calm guide for parents of quiet 3 and 4 year olds: what varies, what deserves a professional look, and what helps at home.

Should you worry if your 3-year-old is not talking in sentences?

Sentences of four or more words are on the CDC’s list of milestones by 4, not 3.[1] By 3, most children put two or more words together, like “Doggie run”, hold a short back-and-forth conversation and are understood by others most of the time.[2][3][4] If your child isn’t doing these yet, or has lost words, talk to your child’s doctor.[4]

Khelu is a toy, not a therapy or treatment. If you’re worried about your child’s speech, talk to your paediatrician.

At a birthday party, another child is narrating a whole film, and yours points and says “come”. Is that fine? Or is it something?

Here is what varies between children, what deserves a professional look, and what helps at home either way.

What varies from child to child?

The milestones from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) sit at the age by which at least 3 in 4 children reach each one.[3] So they describe most children, not every child.

The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) says its own milestone chart “is not a screening or diagnostic tool”. It is meant to start a conversation with your child’s doctor.[5]

And watch your child at home, where they are most at ease, not only at a party.

Two languages at home? Count every word

Asked whether using more than one language can cause a speech or language disorder, the American Academy of Pediatrics and ASHA answer no: “Speech or language problems are not caused by learning multiple languages.”[6]

A child learning two languages may split their words across them. Counted together, the total should be about the same as for a child of the same age learning one language.[6] So count the words in every language your child uses, and keep them all going.

When is it worth a professional look?

Talk to your child’s doctor if:

  • at 3, your child doesn’t yet put two or more words together, with one action word, like “Doggie run”;[2]
  • at 3, your child doesn’t yet keep up a short conversation with you, of at least two back-and-forth exchanges;[4]
  • at 3, other people still can’t understand your child most of the time;[4]
  • by 4, your child doesn’t yet say sentences of four or more words;[1]
  • by 4, people can’t yet understand most of what your child says;[7]
  • at any age, your child loses words or skills they used to have.[4]

These are US guides, and every child is different. Missing one is a reason to ask, not a diagnosis.

The CDC suggests you share your concerns with your child’s doctor and ask about developmental screening.[4] ASHA suggests an assessment by an audiologist, who checks hearing, or a speech-language pathologist if a child misses many of the milestones for their age.[7]

If your gut says ask, ask.

What helps at home, either way

Whatever the milestones say, keep the back-and-forth going. Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child says back-and-forth exchanges between a young child and a caring adult support early language and social skills.[8]

  • Say what you’re doing while you do it, in short sentences: “Shoes on. This foot. Now that one.”
  • Offer a choice instead of a yes-or-no question: “Idli or dosa?” A choice asks for a word.
  • When your child says a word, say it back with one more: “Bus.” “Big bus.”
  • After you ask, wait. Count to five in your head before you help.
  • Read the same book again, and stop before a word your child knows.
  • Sing rhymes and leave the last word for your child.
  • Turn off the TV in the background while you talk and play.

None of this needs a purchase. Grandparents, cousins and older children can all take a turn.
