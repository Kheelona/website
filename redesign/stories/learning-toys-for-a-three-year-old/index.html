Learning toys for a 3-year-old, skill by skill

What a 3-year-old is working on, and which kinds of toys serve each part of it, without the word “educational” doing all the work.

What are the best learning toys for a 3-year-old?

Toys that get your child building, pretending and talking: blocks, simple puzzles, pretend-play sets, crayons and dough, and picture books you read together. Research links back-and-forth talk between a young child and a caring adult to early language and brain development,[1] so the best toys are often the ones you play with together, out loud.

The word “educational” on a box says little about the toy inside. A shape sorter is educational. So is a tablet full of phonics videos. They are not the same purchase.

A better question: what is my 3-year-old working on right now, and does this toy help or get in the way? Here is that list, skill by skill.

What is a 3-year-old working on?

India’s National Education Policy 2020 treats ages 3 to 8 as the Foundational Stage: three years of Anganwadi or pre-school, then Grades 1 and 2.[2]

For these early years, it describes learning that is “play-based, activity-based, and inquiry-based”, covering languages, numbers and counting, colours and shapes, puzzles, drawing, craft, drama and puppetry, music and movement.[3]

It is written for Anganwadis and pre-schools, not for toy shops. Here is what those kinds of play look like at home, skill by skill.

Language: does the toy make a turn?

Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child says back-and-forth “serve and return” exchanges between a young child and a caring adult help build and strengthen neural connections in the child’s brain. They also support the early language and social skills that later, more complex thinking builds on.[1]

In a 2018 study of 36 children aged 4 to 6, children who had more back-and-forth conversational turns with adults showed more activity in Broca’s area, a language region of the brain, while listening to a story. The number of adult words they heard showed no such link. The study shows a link, not cause and effect.[4]

So the test for a language toy is simple: does it make a turn? A picture book you read together makes plenty. A puppet makes as many as you have patience for. A toy that plays a recorded sentence at the press of a button can’t answer back, however many words the sentence has.

Try this: when you read together, pause once or twice and ask what your child thinks will happen next.

Taking turns: games with a “your go, my go” rhythm

Look for games where your child has to wait, watch and then go: simple board games with two rules, picture matching, rolling a ball back and forth. Play them together and say the turns out loud: “My go. Now yours.”

Pretend play: props that leave the story to your child

A cardboard kitchen, a doctor set, a box of mismatched figures. Open props let your child decide who is cooking and what is wrong with teddy. A toy kitchen that plays its own sounds and names its own characters decides some of that for them.

Hands: plain things do the job

Threading beads, stacking cups, dough, chunky crayons, tongs and a bowl of pom-poms. None of them needs a battery or a screen.

Numbers: count real things together

Count spoons at dinner, steps to the door, autos at the signal. Ask, “Which pile has more?” Any toy with parts you can count will do.

Brain development toys for a 3-year-old: what the label means

The words “brain development” on a box prove nothing on their own. What matters is whether a toy gets your child trying, asking and explaining, and whether you talk together while they play.

Harvard’s centre describes the same back-and-forth from infancy: exchanges between a young child and a caring adult that help build and strengthen the connections in the child’s brain.[1] So for a younger child the rule is the same: fewer toys that perform, more time playing and talking together.

What about screens?

For three and four year olds, the World Health Organization and the Indian Academy of Pediatrics both say no more than an hour of screen time a day, and less is better.[5][6]

The American Academy of Pediatrics asks parents to watch with their children, help them understand what they are seeing, and switch screens off an hour before bedtime.[7]

When your child does watch, sit with them and talk about it.

A short rule for the toy shop

  • Check the age on the box.
  • Prefer toys that wait for your child over toys that perform at them.
  • Prefer open ends over scripts.
  • Prefer anything that makes a turn.
