Serve and return: how children learn by talking

What happens when your child asks and you answer, what the research says about it, and how it looks at home.

Talking and languageThe Kheelona team · First published 28 Jul 2026 · 3 min read

What is serve and return?

Serve and return is the back-and-forth between a young child and a caring adult.[1] Your child serves with a look, a word or a question. You return it with attention and a reply. Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child says these exchanges help build neural connections in the child’s brain and support early language and social skills.[1]

Khelu is a toy, not a therapy or treatment. If you’re worried about your child’s speech, talk to your paediatrician.

Your child asks why the moon follows the car. You answer, and before you’ve finished, the next question is on its way: “Then why doesn’t it follow the bus?”

Your child served. You returned. They served again. It happens all day: at breakfast, in the auto, at bath time.

What does the research say?

Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child also calls serve and return, the back-and-forth between young children and the significant adults in their lives, “one of the most essential experiences in shaping the architecture of the developing brain”.[2]

In a 2018 study of 36 children aged 4 to 6, children who had more back-and-forth conversational turns with adults showed more activity in Broca’s area, a language region of the brain, while listening to a story. The number of adult words they heard showed no such link. Turns also predicted the children’s language scores once family income and parents’ education were taken into account.[3]

The study shows a link, not cause and effect.[3] In the study, a turn was an adult speaking and the child replying, or the other way round.[3] A turn needs both of you.

What does it look like at home?

  • At breakfast. Your child holds up a half-eaten dosa: “It’s a moon!” You: “A half moon. What else on your plate is round?”
  • In the auto. “Why is he honking?” You: “What do you think?” Wait for the answer, then add one thing you know.
  • At bath time. Your child pours water from cup to cup. You: “Full. Now empty.” They say it back, and you wait for what comes next.
  • At bedtime. Before you turn the page: “What do you think she’ll do?”

You can do it anywhere: answer your child, then ask one back.

Three habits that keep it going

  • Notice the serve. It can be a point, a question or a tug at your sleeve. Turn towards it when you can.
  • Wait. Give your child time to take their turn before you take yours.
  • Put your phone face down while you talk.

Who can return a serve?

Anyone who cares for your child. Harvard’s Center describes this back-and-forth as a relationship “between children and their parents and other caregivers in the family or community”.[4] In your home, that might be a long list: Nani, Dada, an older cousin, the auntie next door.

Each of them can talk in the language they know best. ASHA, the US association of speech and hearing professionals, says a strong model in any language helps build a foundation for learning others.[5]

No toy or screen replaces the people who talk with your child every day.
