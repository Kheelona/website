Does a three-year-old need an AI tutor?

Tutoring apps promise homework help. Your child can’t do homework yet. What help at 3 should look like.

Does a 3-year-old need an AI tutor?

No. A 3-year-old isn’t ready for homework yet. Help at 3 is simpler: someone who answers their questions, asks one back and waits for the reply. Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child says back-and-forth exchanges like these, with a caring adult, help build a young child’s brain and support early language.[1] Look for play that invites talk, not drills.

What does help look like at 3?

A tutor, in the usual sense, helps a child keep up with school: homework, practice, tests. A 3-year-old doesn’t need any of those. What they have is questions.

Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child says back-and-forth “serve and return” exchanges between a young child and a caring adult help build and strengthen neural connections in the child’s brain. They also support the early language and social skills that later, more complex thinking builds on.[1]

At 3, the serve is often a question. A good return answers it, then asks one back:

Your child: Why is the dosa round?

You: Because we spread the batter round and round. What shape would you make?

Your child: A star!

You: How would we flip a star?

Four turns, and your child did half the thinking. That is what help looks like at 3: a patient grown-up who answers the fourth “why” as kindly as the first.

Try this: after your child’s next “why”, answer in a sentence, then ask, “What do you think?” Count to five before you say more.

What about a tutoring app on a tablet?

A tutoring app on a phone or tablet is screen time.

For three and four year olds, the Indian Academy of Pediatrics says no more than an hour of screen time a day, for no more than 20 to 30 minutes at a time, always supervised by a caregiver.[2] The World Health Organization’s limit for watching screens at this age is the same hour, and it adds that less is better.[3]

That hour covers every screen in the day, so a tutoring app shares it with everything else. From five to ten, the IAP’s limit is under two hours a day, and it counts screen time for schoolwork too.[2]

If a screen is part of your child’s day, sit with them and talk about what you see.

What about AI that does the thinking for them?

In a May 2026 commentary for the Brookings Institution, Rebecca Winthrop, who directs its Center for Universal Education, described one worry about children and AI as “cognitive stunting”: relying too heavily on AI “reduces children’s opportunities to engage in effortful thinking”.[4]

She calls the evidence emerging, and none of the findings she reports is about children as young as 3.[4]

Whatever that evidence shows in time, the habit is easy to keep at home: give your child time to think before anyone, or anything, gives the answer. That is the case for a question back. It hands the thinking to your child.

What to look for instead

If you are weighing an AI toy or app for a young child, look for these:

  • Talk, not taps: your child speaks and listens, with no screen to watch.
  • It answers, then asks your child something back.
  • It says when it doesn’t know, instead of guessing.
  • It can’t browse the open internet.
  • You can read every conversation.
