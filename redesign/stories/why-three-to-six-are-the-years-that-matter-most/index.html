Why the early years matter most

What the early years are for, and the ordinary things that fill them well: talk, play and sleep.

Why do the early years matter so much?

Because so much is built in them. Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child calls the early years the most active period for building connections in the brain,[1] and says back-and-forth exchanges with a caring adult help build them.[2] What fills these years well is ordinary: talk, play and sleep, with the people who love your child.

None of it needs a class or a kit. Here is what is being built, and the three ordinary things that fill these years well.

What happens in a young child’s brain?

Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child estimates that more than a million new connections form in the brain every second in the first few years of life.[1] The early years are the most active period for building those connections, but new connections can form throughout life.[1]

India’s National Curriculum Framework for the Foundational Stage says brain development is most rapid in the first eight years of a child’s life.[3] So if your child is between 3 and 7, they are still inside those years.

Both describe how busy these years are. Neither is a deadline.

Talk: answer, then ask one back

Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child says back-and-forth “serve and return” exchanges between a young child and a caring adult help build and strengthen neural connections in the child’s brain. They also support the early language and social skills that later, more complex thinking builds on.[2]

Between 3 and 7, a serve is often a question, a story or a complaint about a sibling. Your return is your attention and a reply. Try ending it with a question back, then wait.

For more ways to do this every day, read our guide to what actually builds a sharp brain.

Play, and plenty of it

India’s National Education Policy 2020 treats ages 3 to 8 as the Foundational Stage of school education: three years of Anganwadi or pre-school, then Grades 1 and 2.[4] It describes those five years as “flexible, multilevel, play/activity-based learning”.[4]

At home, play needs very little: a pretend shop, a cardboard box that becomes a bus, a tower built only to be knocked down. Let your child lead, and join in when you’re asked.

Sleep, the part that’s easiest to cut

Children aged 3 to 5 need 10 to 13 hours of sleep in every 24, naps included, and children aged 6 to 12 need 9 to 12 hours.[5]

Late dinners, family functions and screens all eat into it. So keep bedtime steady, most nights.

What these years don’t need

A packed timetable. Flashcards before breakfast. The worry that you have already missed something.

What fills these years well is ordinary: talk, play and sleep, most days, with the people who love your child.
