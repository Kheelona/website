Screen time for a 3-year-old, and up to age 7

What the WHO, the Indian Academy of Pediatrics and the AAP say, age by age, and what to do at 6 pm.

How much screen time for a 3-year-old?

No more than an hour a day, and less is better. The World Health Organization and the Indian Academy of Pediatrics both say so for three and four year olds.[1][2] What the screen replaces matters too: talking, play, sleep and moving about. When your child does watch, sit with them and talk about it.

That hour is a ceiling, not a target.

Below: what changes at each age up to 7, and what to do when the evening gets hard.

How much screen time for a 4-year-old?

The same hour. The WHO and the IAP set it for four year olds too.[1][2] The American Academy of Pediatrics sets no more than an hour a day of high-quality programmes for children aged two to five, watched together.[3]

For three and four year olds, the IAP adds two details: no more than 20 to 30 minutes at a time, and a grown-up always supervising.[2]

How much screen time for a 5-year-old?

Under two hours a day, says the IAP, from 5 until 10, and that includes time on a screen for homework. Less is better.[2] The WHO’s guideline stops at 4.[1]

How much screen time at 6 and 7?

The IAP’s limit of under two hours a day still applies, homework included.[2] The AAP’s advice for children aged 5 to 18 sets no fixed number of hours: it asks families to place consistent limits on time and on the kinds of media used.[4]

Agree the limit with your child, keep it the same every day, and keep screens from crowding out sleep and play.

What if your child watches more than that?

You are not alone. Across 10 studies from India, covering 2,857 children under five, children averaged about 2.2 hours of screen time a day. That is more than the guidelines advise.[5]

Cut back a little at a time, and have something ready to fill the gap. Our seven-day plan, The gentle way off the phone, shows one way to do it.

What the hour leaves room for

The AAP says the one-hour limit for young children is there to leave them “ample time to engage in other activities important to their health and development”.[3] And when a young child is sitting still, the WHO encourages reading and storytelling with a caregiver.[1]

So look at the whole day, not just the minutes. Is there room for talking, playing, moving about and sleep?

Does it matter what they watch?

Yes. The AAP says well-designed programmes “can improve cognitive, literacy, and social outcomes for children 3 to 5 years of age”.[3] It also asks parents to avoid three things: fast-paced programmes (young children do not understand them as well), apps with lots of distracting content, and anything violent.[3]

And it asks parents to watch with their children and help them understand what they are seeing.[3]

Try this: after an episode, ask your child to tell you what happened, and which part they liked best.

What about meals and bedtime?

For children under five, the IAP and the AAP both say no screens at meals and none in the hour before sleep.[2][3] The AAP also asks families to keep bedrooms screen-free.[3] The IAP adds car, bus and train journeys to the screen-free list.[2]

If your evening wind-down leans on a screen, move the screen earlier and put a bath, a story or a song in its place.

What do you do at 6 pm?

Knowing the number does not cook dinner. A few habits worth trying:

  • Decide when screen time ends before it starts, and tell your child.
  • End at a natural break, such as the end of an episode, not halfway through.
  • Put something your child enjoys straight after it, so the end is also a start.
  • Keep the screen in a room you share, not the bedroom.
  • Sit with them for a few minutes of it, and talk about what you see.
