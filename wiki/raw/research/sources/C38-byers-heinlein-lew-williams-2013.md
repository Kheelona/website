# Byers-Heinlein and Lew-Williams (2013), LEARNing Landscapes

- **Citation:** Krista Byers-Heinlein (Concordia University) and Casey Lew-Williams (Northwestern
  University), "Bilingualism in the Early Years: What the Science Says", *LEARNing Landscapes*
  7(1):95-112, Autumn 2013. PMCID PMC6168212, PMID 30288204, NIHMS940156.
- **What was read:** the full text of the authors' accepted manuscript on PubMed Central (labelled
  "Author manuscript; available in PMC: 2018 Oct 2"). Plain GET, 2026-10-11 05:56 UTC
  (2026-10-10 local), HTTP 200. Local copy outside the repo:
  `scratchpad/sources/language-bilingual/pmc6168212.txt`.
- **What kind of source:** a narrative review written for parents and the people they ask for advice
  (the article names preschool and elementary teachers, pediatricians and speech-language
  pathologists). It organises the research around six parent questions. It is not a new study.

## Exact passage and locators

**Locator: section "6. Are bilingual children more likely to have language difficulties, delays, or
disorders?", first sentence.**
> "Bilingual children are not more likely than monolingual children to have difficulties with language"

The same sentence goes on to say: nor more likely to show delays in learning or to be diagnosed with a
language disorder (citing Paradis, Genesee and Crago 2010; Petitto and Holowka 2002).

**Paraphrased, same paragraph (not quoted):** bilingual children typically know fewer words in each
language than monolingual learners of that language. The gap disappears when "conceptual vocabulary"
is counted: add the words known in each language, without double-counting translation pairs such as
dog and perro, and bilingual children know approximately the same number of words as monolingual
children (Pearson, Fernandez and Oller 1993; Pearson and Fernandez 1994). The next paragraph says
remaining differences in one language "are likely to become less noticeable with time".

**Locator: section "1. Are bilingual children confused?", paragraph 2.** Paraphrased: code mixing is
a normal part of bilingual development; children borrow a word from the other language when they do
not know or cannot retrieve it; and even 2-year-olds show "some ability" to modulate their language
to the language their conversational partner uses (Genesee, Boivin and Nicoladis 1996).

## What this source does and does not support

- Supports: no higher rate of language difficulty, delay or disorder; fewer words per language but
  about the same conceptual total; code mixing is normal; some 2-year-olds already adjust language to
  the listener.
- Does NOT say: that mixing "fades as each language fills out" (not stated); that the total "matches"
  exactly (it says "approximately the same", and counts translation pairs once); that the authors are
  "two developmental psychologists" (the bios give Psychology at Concordia and Communication Sciences
  and Disorders at Northwestern; the authors call themselves "researchers who study bilingual infants
  and children").
- Population: mostly North American and European research on infants and toddlers; India is named
  only as a region where language contact is typical.
