- **Citation:** Paul Bloom (Department of Psychology, Yale University), "Precis of *How Children
  Learn the Meanings of Words*", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 24(6), 2001, pp. 1095-1103.

## Exact passage and locator

**Locator: p.1095, first paragraph of the article after the epigraph.** From the estimate that the
average English-speaking 17-year-old knows more than 60,000 words, and that first words come at
about the first birthday, Bloom concludes:

> "this comes to over ten new words per day"

## What this source does and does not support

- Supports, as an average: children learn new words at a rate that works out to more than ten a day
  across childhood and adolescence (ages about 1 to 17), for English speakers.
- Does NOT give a rate for ages 3 to 6, and does not say the "collection explodes" between three
  and six. It is back-of-envelope arithmetic from a vocabulary total, not a count of words learned
  per day by preschoolers.
- Does NOT say anything about vocabulary doubling between three and four.
- **Not read:** Madorah Smith's 1925 University of Iowa dissertation, "An investigation of the
  development of the sentence and the extent of vocabulary in young children" (Iowa Research Online,
  public domain, 131 leaves), the classic age-by-age vocabulary study, was located but its PDF was
  not opened (the repository's viewer did not open and the file was not downloaded). No figures
  from it are recorded here.
