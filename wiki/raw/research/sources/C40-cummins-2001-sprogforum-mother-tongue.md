- **Citation:** Jim Cummins (University of Toronto), "Bilingual Children's Mother Tongue: Why is it

- **What kind of source:** a short synthesis and position article by a leading researcher on
  bilingual education, for educators. It summarises research (citing Baker 2000, Cummins 2000,
  Skutnabb-Kangas 2000); it is not a study. Its subject is school-age children and schooling.

## Exact passage and locator

**Locator: p.18, the paragraph beginning "We can understand how this happens".** Pupils who can tell
the time in their mother tongue, to tell time in a second language:

> "they do not need to re-learn the concept of telling time"

and only need new labels ("surface structures") for a skill they already have (paraphrase of the
rest of the sentence).

**Paraphrased, pp.17-18, paragraph beginning "The level of development of children's mother tongue
is a strong predictor of their second language development" (that opening is quoted):** children who
start school with a solid mother-tongue foundation develop stronger literacy in the school language;
when parents and grandparents tell stories and discuss things in the mother tongue, children come to
school well prepared; knowledge and skills transfer across languages, and for concepts and thinking
skills the two languages are interdependent.

**Paraphrased, p.19:** children's mother tongues are fragile and easily lost in the early school
years, sometimes within 2-3 years of starting school.

## What this source does and does not support

- Supports, as an expert's argument: concepts learned in the mother tongue do not need relearning in
  a second language, only new words; a strong mother tongue predicts stronger second-language
  literacy.
- Does NOT say relabelling is "far faster"; gives no speed. "Strong predictor" is an association,
  not proof of cause. It is about pupils and school literacy, not measured in 3-year-olds at home.
