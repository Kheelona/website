# C35: the study that named "screen time tantrums"

**Source 1 (the paper).** Alexis Hiniker, Hyewon Suh, Sabina Cao, Julie A. Kientz (University of
Washington). "Screen Time Tantrums: How Families Manage Screen Media Experiences for Toddlers and
Preschoolers." *Proceedings of the 2016 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems*,
pages 648-660, May 2016. DOI 10.1145/2858036.2858278 (Crossref metadata confirmed 2026-10-10).

**Read: the ABSTRACT only.** The ACM Digital Library page returned HTTP 403 to curl and WebFetch on
2026-10-10. The abstract was read from the Internet Archive's 2021-02-12 capture of the ACM page
(URL in front matter). Unpaywall and Semantic Scholar list no open copy; Semantic Scholar marks it
CLOSED. **The full paper, and so its own percentages, were NOT read.**

**Source 2 (the university's release about the paper).** University of Washington News, "Two-minute
warnings make kids’ ‘screen time’ tantrums worse", Jennifer Langston, May 5, 2016. Fetched
2026-10-10 by GET. This is the authors' institution describing the study; it is not the paper.

## Exact passages

Paper, abstract (ACM page, archived):
> "We investigate children's (age 1 - 5) transitions to and from screen-based activities to
> understand the boundaries families have set and their experiences living within them. We report
> on interviews with 27 parents and a diary study with a separate 28 families examining these
> transitions."

> "Transitioning away from screens is often painful, and predictive factors determine the pain of
> a transition. Technology-mediated transitions are significantly more successful than
> parent-mediated transitions"

UW News release:
> "Those answers informed a diary study in which 28 different families documented screen time
> experiences over the course of two weeks [...]"

> "Surprisingly, 59 percent of the time parents reported that their child had a neutral reaction
> to ending screen time. Another 19 percent of the transitions evoked a positive reaction, and 22
> percent evoked a negative reaction."

> "“Most of the time these transitions actually go pretty smoothly, which can be hard for parents
> to recognize,” said senior author and associate professor of human-centered design and
> engineering Julie Kientz ."

> "But children aged 1 to 5 who were given a two-minute warning by their parents were more upset
> when the screen went away."

## Notes for copy

- The paper's own abstract supports "often painful" for children aged 1 to 5 in a US sample.
- The university's release gives the diary numbers: about one transition in five (22 percent) was
  negative, and most were neutral or positive. That cuts against "so common" if read as "most of
  the time".
- A 2024 paper (see `C35-gavrilova-veraksa-2024-screen-time-tantrums.md`) cites this study for
  "over 90% of families". That figure was NOT found in the abstract or the release and could not be
  checked against the paywalled paper; do not use it.
