# Photography, chalk & crayon

Kheelona pictures have three layers, used together or apart:

1. **The photo:** a real child in a real Indian home.
2. **The Imagination layer:** chalk or ink line drawings of what the child is thinking about.
3. **The scrapbook layer:** crayon marks, cut-paper shapes and stickers that hold the layout together.

## 1. Photography

**The look:** a warm afternoon at home, photographed by a parent who happens to be good with a camera. Across a campaign, think of a portrait studio: **the same child, 100% the same face, in different clothes and different rooms**, never the same outfit in the same spot.

**Settings: home, studio or plain background only.** Every photo is set in a real Indian home, on a seamless studio sweep (cream or white), or against a plain wall. No beaches, parks, streets, malls or other outdoor locations: they don't sell a learning toy, they date quickly, and they compete with the child and Kheelu for attention. The 25 approved photos in the **Photo library** group follow this rule; start there before making anything new (see **The cast**).

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Real upper-middle-class Indian homes: cotton curtains, steel tumblers, a dhurrie, tiled or stone floors, a bookshelf, plants, a little clutter. Or a plain cream sweep / plain wall for cut-out-ready portraits | Outdoor locations (beach, sea, park, street), showrooms, Western-only cues (fireplaces, carpeted suburbs) |
| Window light from one side with soft natural shadow | Ring lights, flat front flash, HDR, glowing rim light |
| Camera at the child's eye level, close and kind | Looking down at children, wide-angle distortion |
| Warm, matte film colour (Portra-like), fine grain, true skin tones | Glossy skin, airbrushing, orange-and-teal grading, heavy blur |
| Mid-gesture: asking, laughing, counting on fingers, whispering to Kheelu | Stiff catalogue smiles, adult-like poses, thumbs up |
| Imperfection: flyaway hair, creased cotton, a crayon smudge, a missing tooth | Perfect symmetry, spotless rooms, matching outfits |
| A full range of Indian skin tones, from wheatish to deep brown | Lightened skin, "fair" bias in casting, filters that whiten |
| Kheelu in hand, on a lap or sitting beside the child, actually engaged | Kheelu floating, cropped through the face, or ignored as a prop |

**Shot list for any campaign**

- **Hero:** child and Kheelu together, eye contact or a shared moment, with space for a headline (negative space on the left or top).
- **Detail:** small hands on Kheelu's belly buttons; ears in a child's fist; the light ring glowing.
- **Parent proof:** a parent reading the app in the background while the child plays in focus.
- **Moments of the day:** morning "why", after playschool, homework and counting time, festive evenings, evening songs, bedtime (Bedtime theme). All at home.
- **Cut-out ready:** the same child against a plain wall or the cream studio sweep, so the photo can be cut out as a sticker.
- **Without Kheelu:** two in every five library photos show the child alone (drawing, building, reading, a portrait), so pages and decks can talk about childhood without always selling.

**Composition:** keep 30–40% of the frame calm for text. Crop at natural joints (waist, mid-thigh), never at the wrist, knee or neck. Keep faces off the edge of 4:5 and 9:16 crops.

## 2. The Imagination layer (our signature)

**The idea:** Kheelu is intelligent because of what it sparks in a child, not because of the technology. So we never draw the AI. We draw the child's idea: a chalk drawing appears around the child, as if the conversation has spilled onto a blackboard.

> Kabir asks, "how do astronauts sleep?" A chalk astronaut helmet appears around his head, with a rocket, a planet and two stars.

**Rules**

- **Chalk on dark, ink on light.** Use `chalk` lines on the ink "chalkboard" block (`ink-900`) or on a dark area of a photo. On light photos and fur grounds, use the ink versions. Lines must keep 3:1 contrast against what is directly behind them.
- **Draw the topic, not the tech.** Draw astronauts, mangoes, trains, numbers, rainbows, musical notes, the story's dragon. Never draw circuit lines, chips, brains, sparkle stars around Kheelu, Wi-Fi waves or "AI" glows.
- **Put the question in the child's words**, in Kalam at the top of the frame, lower case, with no quotation marks: "how do astronauts sleep?"
- Use **three to five doodles**, and make one of them the hero: the helmet, the crown, the thought cloud. More than five turns the photo into wallpaper.
- **Never cover the face.** Lines may circle the head (helmet, crown) but never cross the eyes, nose or mouth.
- **Wear it, don't perch it.** A helmet goes on like a real one: the bubble holds the whole head, from just above the hair to below the chin, with the ear pods level with the ears and the collar ring at the neck. A crown sits on the hair. A helmet resting on top of the head, with its rim across the eyes, reads as a hat that doesn't fit.
- **Kheelu stays in front.** When the child holds Kheelu up near their face, the doodle goes behind Kheelu (the `clip` option in `ImaginationFrame`), never across it.
- **One child per frame**, the one doing the asking.
- Keep the drawings in the hand-drawn style of the Doodles group (rough, double-stroked, slightly grainy). New doodles must match: a 3–4.5px rough stroke at 1080px wide, round caps, no fills, no gradients.

**Ready-made doodles** (Doodles group, chalk and ink): astronaut helmet, rocket, planet, star, sun, rainbow, crown, heart, music notes, open book, thought cloud, speech bubble, question mark, spark lines, swirl, curly arrow. The `ImaginationFrame` component places them by percentage, so they stay put at any size.

**Where to use it:** hero sections about learning, social posts about curiosity, deck slides about the product's intelligence, and the "How it works" story. **Where not to use it:** safety, pricing, the app's data screens. Those stay plain and factual.

## 3. The scrapbook layer

**Stickers.** Cut-out children and Kheelu with a white `chalk` outline (`stroke-sticker`) and `shadow-sticker`, placed on a brand-colour block, like the Kheelona Cast stickers in the Cast group. A sticker may overlap the block's edge or the next sticker by up to 15%. Kid stickers are cropped flat at the waist, so place them on the bottom edge of the frame.

**Crayon marks** (Crayon group, or the `Mark` helper in code), in the three brand colours:

| Mark | Use for |
|---|---|
| `highlight-sun` | A marker swipe behind one key word in a headline |
| `underline-chilli`, `underline-blue`, `underline-sun` | One word or a short phrase in a headline or slide (sun is a double line) |
| `circle-chilli`, `circle-blue` | Circling a number or price ("₹499") |
| `squiggle-blue`, `zigzag-chilli` | Edge decoration on social posts, used sparingly |
| `scribble-sun` | A playful corner on kid-facing material |
| `burst-sun` | Behind the product on a plain ground |

Use one mark per headline. Never put marks under body copy, in the app, or behind prices in the checkout.

**Cut-paper shapes** (Shapes group): cloud and blob (blue), flower, half-round and heart (chilli), sun and star (sunshine), ribbon (blue), and the rainbow arch, the one shape in Kheelu's ear colours. They go behind products and children as a soft stage, at most two per view, and they never touch text.

## Inspiration we drew from

- Toy-store social grids that combine full-colour blocks, a bold rounded headline with one handwritten word, children cut out against colour, and one repeated footer pill. That gave us **SocialPost**.
- Children's brand books built on crayon textures, cut-paper shapes, sticker outlines and handwritten notes on photos. That gave us **the scrapbook layer**.
- A school campaign photo of a boy with a chalk astronaut drawn around him. That gave us **the Imagination layer**.

These references are not ours. Never copy their layouts, characters or type.

## AI-generated imagery

AI images are allowed only by the protocol in **Making new assets (for agents)**: cast references, real Kheelu reference, film-photo prompts and human review. Every AI image must pass the "would a parent believe this is a photo of a real home?" test, and must never pass as a real customer.
